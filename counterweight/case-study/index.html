decided in the first review, found a year after handover

the costly problems in a building are rarely the dramatic ones. they are the glass nobody can reach to clean, the bin route through the lobby, the canopy that drums under a bedroom window.

take the bins

at an early review, a team compares six options on daylight, views, area and how each one looks in a render. the refuse store goes wherever it fits. nobody draws the route from the store to the kerb, because nothing in the review asks for it. a year after handover, the caretaker wheels every bin through the entrance lobby, and moving the store now means moving the building.

a composite, not a real project: it is the pattern post-occupancy studies keep finding.3

~60%

of 18,704 latent defects in 74 buildings could have been prevented by better design, against 33% by better workmanship.1

6.9% + 7.4%

of contract value lost to design errors, in direct and then indirect costs, across 139 projects.2

same faults

recur from building to building, because teams move on and feedback rarely reaches designers.3

and ai is arriving

  • uk practices using ai41% in 2024, 59% in 20254
  • generated imagessystematically break projective geometry5
  • ai explanationsraise acceptance whether or not the ai is right6
  • llm compliance checkersgive different answers to the same question10

early options now arrive as anything from a bim model to an ai render, and ai is entering practice faster than anyone can check it. so the question became:

how do you compare six options honestly when one is a bim model, two are massings, one is a scanned plan and two are ai renders?

what architects need from a tool

architects already have more software than they want, and most of it asks them to change how they work. a new tool earns a place only if it fits how a design review actually runs. every screen was checked against these six needs.

stay in the model

nobody re-models a scheme to ask one question about it, and an export ritual is skipped the week it matters.

so counterweight is a widget beside rhino, revit or whatever the office draws in, and reads what is already there. an ifc file dropped on it becomes another option.

meet the stage you are at

when the big decisions are made, the options are massings, sketches, a scanned plan and a competition render. a tool that needs a full bim model arrives after the decisions.

so it takes every kind of input and says what each one can answer, rather than refusing the ones that are not finished.

hold up in the room

a review is an argument between architect, client, planner and consultants. a finding someone cannot challenge does not survive it.

so every number points at a place in the model and carries its tolerance and its source. nothing has to be taken on trust.

leave the judgement alone

trading overlook against daylight, or a longer bin route against a better courtyard, is the architect's job, not the software's.

so it shows who leads each check and by how much. there is no total and no winner.

speak practice

facades, routes, the lobby, the kerb. not risk scores, and not sentences that claim more than a drawing shows.

so rooms are found by the names offices already give them, and every sentence the tool writes describes a condition, never an experience.

end in a drawing request

when something cannot be answered yet, the useful output is what to ask the team for next.

so gaps become requests: "a ground-floor plan for stepped block would answer two more checks."

it is not a compliance checker, not generative design and not a replacement for the review. it is the second pair of eyes in it.

these needs come from published research on post-occupancy feedback and ai trust, and from informal conversations with architects. none were recorded, so no one is quoted; recorded sessions are the first item under what's next.

the tool i built and threw away

first direction

a ledger that put a price on everything: money, disruption, people and risk for every move, payback periods, a composite score and a recommended fix on every finding.

what happened

it looked rigorous and was not. where evidence was missing, it quietly turned the gap into a number, and a render and a bim model fed the same score. no architect could have defended it to a client, and none should have.

what changed

every value is now marked by what kind of evidence it is. ranges stay ranges, images never become measurements, there is no winner and no invented cost. if nothing can answer a check, it stays open and says what would.

the honest version is smaller. it is also the only one an architect could defend in a room.

four kinds of evidence

a typical ai checker takes any file and returns an answer. counterweight first marks each input by what kind of evidence it actually is. only numbers can be ranked, and only against a tolerance.

measured

read from geometry the input contains. one number.

9.4 m

estimated

a range from stated assumptions, such as a scale bar drawn by hand.

11.3–12.8 m

observed

seen in an image. shown with the crop it came from, never turned into a number.

full-height glazing

not in input

the file cannot answer. neutral, out of every comparison, and it says what would answer it.

needs elevations

one chart that will not pick a winner

the overview has one job: show, for seven checks at once, which option leads each one and whether that lead is real. i drew it five ways. four of them made a claim the evidence could not support.

radar chart

the filled area reads as a total score, and every axis needs the same scale. metres, shares and counts do not share one.

rejected: implies a winner

heatmap

good for scanning, but colour has to mean the same thing in every row. normalising each row hides whether a difference is inside the tolerance.

kept for the evidence tab

bars per check

honest per check, but seven charts to scan, and a 0.4 m lead looks as real as a 4 m one.

kept inside each open check

ranked list

ranks hide ties. second place by 0.1 m reads like a loss, and a missing input quietly falls to the bottom.

rejected: hides ties

the map

one chart for every check. direction is who leads, distance is the lead divided by that check's tolerance, so units stop mattering and ties have a place to sit.

chosen

the cost: the map shows only the leader on each check. the full spread of all six options lives in the strip inside each open check, one click away.

  1. 1
    direction is who leadsa check sits on the spoke toward the option its evidence favours.
  2. 2
    distance is by how muchthe lead divided by the check's tolerance, on rings at 2×, 5× and 10×. a 1.2 m lead on overlook and a 6 point lead on glazing can sit on the same chart honestly.
  3. 3
    the fog is too close to callanything within one tolerance stays in the middle, between the two options it cannot separate. the tool will not pick a side.
  4. 4
    a tail means a rangewhen the lead rests on estimates, the dot sits at the smallest lead the evidence allows and a blurred tail runs to the largest.

key design decisions

01

every number points back at the geometry

why

a review argues about places, not figures. a distance nobody can locate cannot be checked or challenged.

tradeoff

every value has to carry a location, which limits what can become a check at all.

result

show in model flies the camera to the exact facades, route or roof, with the value pinned beside it.

02

numbers from ai renders are hidden by default

why

generated images break perspective and shadow geometry.5 a lane width scaled from people in a render looks precise and is not.

tradeoff

a reviewer who wants a rough figure has one more click, and the render rarely ranks.

result

the crop it came from is shown instead. "show the number anyway" reveals it, labelled as a guess.

03

no overall winner, anywhere

why

a total hides the tradeoffs a review exists to argue about, and an explained score makes people agree with it whether or not it is right.6

tradeoff

a review ends without a single answer. that is harder to sell and easier to defend.

result

clicking an option shows where it stands on each check, in words. lead counts per option were removed for reading as a score.

04

the next drawing request writes itself

why

when evidence is missing, the most useful output is what to ask for next, not a blank.

tradeoff

gaps are ranked by how many checks they would answer, not by which checks matter most on this project.

result

"a ground-floor plan for stepped block would answer two more checks and make both comparable." ai references are never asked for drawings.

smaller decisions

  • sync is a button, not livea review compares fixed versions, and geometry mid-edit is often broken
  • a sync shows what movedreviews repeat, so the change is the news, not the whole table again
  • looking is not leaving outclick inspects; the eye, or shift-click, removes an option
  • a failed sync keeps the last good comparisonand the status says so

language

geometry proves a condition, not an experience. every sentence the tool writes was checked against that line.

this canopy will disturb residents when it rains.

a canopy is directly below these windows. whether rain is audible depends on roof build-up and room construction.

scheme a fails the overlook standard.

4 facing window pairs are closer than 12 m, your project setting.

two roofs have no drainage.

no drain objects on 2 flat areas. they may be unmodelled rather than missing.

when the answer is "not from this"

early drawings are incomplete by nature, so the most useful answer is often a refusal with a reason. each of these is a capture of the prototype with the comparison rules running, not a mockup.

built to plug into the tools offices already use

counterweight is a widget, not a modelling tool. a thin adapter per design platform does four things, and everything after it is shared. evidence kinds come from what an input contains, never from which platform sent it, so the rules and their tests do not change per platform.

find the alternatives

maps the team's own grouping to options: layers or blocks in rhino, design options in revit, tags in sketchup, or one file each.

send a snapshot

not the model. element ids, categories, geometry, units, and what the input contains: openings, drains, rooms.

watch for edits

listens for the platform's own change events and marks the comparison out of date.

show in model

takes element ids and the measured line or route back, highlights them and moves the view.

what is real

the prototype is a typescript monorepo. the comparison rules sit in a package with no dependencies. every message between widget and platform follows a versioned schema, with json schema generated for adapters written in c# or ruby. the 3d canvas is a stand-in platform behind a real adapter, so the widget cannot tell it from a rhino plugin.

two checks, overlook and the bin route, are measured from the model's own geometry. an edit in the platform is what moves their numbers, and show in model draws the exact line or route that was measured.

an ifc file dropped on the canvas becomes a new option. the adapter turns walls, slabs and roofs into the volumes the readers measure, squares the model to its own grid, keeps a canopy on columns off the ground so bins can pass under it, and finds the bin store, the lobby and the collection point by the names offices already give them.

how it is tested

  • fewer than two comparable inputsopen, nothing scored
  • gap = leader's worst case − runner-up's best caseconservative
  • gap ≤ tolerancetoo close to call
  • observed or not in inputnever ranked
  • number read from an ai referencehidden until asked for

no model decides a verdict, so the same inputs always give the same answer. property tests generate thousands of random cases and assert what must always hold: a range never wins on its best case, the order of options changes nothing, moving the whole model changes nothing, a bin route never cuts through a building.

to test the tests, i planted fifteen bugs, from comparing midpoints instead of worst cases to letting bins walk through walls. all fifteen were caught by the 91 automated tests. the ifc adapter is tested end to end: models written in millimetres, turned off the grid and moved to survey coordinates must read the same as the boxes they were drawn from.

what this does not prove

the tests prove the comparison is consistent and conservative, and that the two readers measure the geometry they are given. they do not prove these are the right checks, or that a real office model arrives clean: layers misnamed, rooms unnamed, a bin store drawn as a line. the other five checks still read recorded, illustrative evidence. before any check is trusted on a real project it needs a written claim, the geometry it requires, the inputs that make it invalid, tests on real models and a reviewer with the right expertise.

reflection

the hardest design work here was deciding what the screen must refuse to say. every version got more trustworthy by showing less, and more useful to the person who has to defend the drawing.
